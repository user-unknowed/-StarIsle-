Allergic diseases are common chronic conditions in childhood, yet the role of adverse childhood
experiences (ACEs) in their development remains incompletely understood at the population
level. Using pooled data from the 2016 –2023 National Survey of Children ’s Health, a nationally
representative survey of U.S. children aged 0 –17 years, we examined associations between
ACEs and parent-reported allergic conditions and asthma, including parent-reported disease
severity (moderate-to-severe). ACEs were assessed across nine parent-reported domains and
summarized as a cumulative score. Weighted logistic regression and restricted cubic spline
models were performed to estimate associations between ACEs and allergic outcomes, with
subgroup analyses by age and sociodemographic characteristics. A total of 290,599 children
were included ( M[SD] age =8.62 [5.16] years; 51.17% male), representing 61.5 million
children. The weighted prevalence was 20.09% for allergic conditions and 7.18% for asthma.
Compared with no ACEs, exposure to ≥4 ACEs was associated with higher odds of parent-
reported allergic conditions ( OR=1.85; 95% CI [1.68, 2.04]) and asthma ( OR=2.08; 95% CI
[1.79, 2.41]). Associations were strongest in early childhood. Among children aged 0 –2 years,
those exposed to ≥4 ACEs had 2.6-fold higher odds of allergic conditions ( OR=2.64; 95% CI
[1.65, 4.23]) and nearly ﬁvefold increased odds of asthma ( OR=4.71; 95% CI [2.24, 9.91]),
compared to those with no ACEs. In item-level analyses, caregiver mental illness and experiences
of racial discrimination showed the strongest associations with allergic outcomes. These ﬁndings
highlight early-life adversity as an important social determinant of allergic disease and the need
for early identi ﬁcation and prevention strategies.
Public Signi ﬁcance Statement
This nationally representative study found that children exposed to more adverse childhood
experiences had higher odds of parent-reported allergic conditions and asthma, with the
strongest associations observed in the youngest children. These ﬁndings suggest that early
adversity may be an important social factor in childhood allergic health and support early
identi ﬁcation and trauma-informed approaches in pediatric allergy and asthma care.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are commonly
conceptualized as early-life exposures to chronic or recurrent
social and familial adversities that shape developmental
trajectories of health across the life course ( Bhutta et al.,
2023 ). Rather than operating through single, linear pathways,
ACEs are increasingly understood to in ﬂuence mental and
physical health through complex, interacting processes that
unfold over time and are shaped by resilience and adaptation
(Hays-Grudo et al., 2021 ). Nearly 60% of children worldwide
have experienced at least one ACE, with over one third
exposed to multiple adversities ( Madigan et al., 2025 ).
Therefore, understanding h ow ACEs relate to diverse
health outcomes is essential for clarifying the develop-
mental roots of lifelong risk.
ACEs and Lifespan Health Outcomes
First proposed by Felitti and colleagues in 1998 ( Felitti et al.,
1998 ), ACEs refer to various domains of potentially traumatic
events occurring in early life, offering a cumulative and
quanti ﬁable framework for assessing their long-term health
consequences. Subsequent decades of research have estab-
lished robust links between ACE exposure and a wide
spectrum of mental health conditions, including depression,
anxiety, and suicidality ( Danelsdo ttir et al., 2024 ;Weems et
al., 2021 ). Over time, growing evidence has extended these
associations to physical health outcomes, such as obesity,
hypertension, and cardiovascular disease ( Bhutta et al., 2023 ;
Moriya et al., 2024 ). However, the relationship between ACEs
and health outcomes is far from deterministic, and consid-
erable uncertainty remains regarding how, when, and in whom
early adversity translates into speci ﬁch e a l t hr i s k s .
Contemporary life-course models, such as the
Intergenerational and Cumulative Adverse and Resilient
Experiences model proposed by Hays-Grudo et al. (2021) ,
emphasize the dynamic interplay among adversity, adaptation,
and resilience in shaping long-term health across critical
developmental windows, from the prenatal period through
childhood. Consistent with this perspective, ACE exposure
does not produce uniform outcomes; rather, the health con-
sequences of similar adversity pro ﬁles may diverge dependingon developmental timing and the availability of buffering
supports. Notably, the immune system is highly plastic during
the prenatal and early postnatal periods, rendering immune-
mediated outcomes particularly sensitive to early-life adver-
sity ( Beyer et al., 2025 ). Maternal experiences of anxiety,
depression, major negative life events, or exposure to violence
during pregnancy have been linked to higher risks of wheeze
and asthma in offspring ( Adgent et al., 2024 ;Rosa et al.,
2018 ). These features underscore the value of allergic
diseases —including, but not limited to, asthma —as mean-
ingful outcomes for understanding the developmental con-
sequences of early adversity from the prenatal period through
Allergic Diseases and Asthma in the Context of ACEs
Children with allergic diseases, including allergic rhinitis,
asthma, atopic dermatitis, and food allergies, represent a large
portion of this burden ( Pawankar, 2014 ;Shin et al., 2023 ).
These conditions are among the most prevalent chronic
diseases in childhood, affecting approximately 10% –30% of
children globally, and their prevalence has been rising in
recent decades ( Koo et al., 2023 ;Pate et al., 2022 ;Shin et
al., 2023 ). In the United States, national data suggest that
approximately one in ﬁve children are now affected, re ﬂecting
a continued rise in parent-reported allergic conditions ( Pate et
al., 2022 ). Beyond symptom burden, pediatric allergic diseases
are linked to missed school days, impaired daily functioning,
and reduced quality of life, and they often cluster develop-
mentally along the “allergic march ”(Dharmage et al., 2022 ;
Ma et al., 2024 ), underscoring the importance of identifying
early-life determinants that shape both onset and severity.
Allergic diseases have also been linked to elevated risks
of depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and suicidality,
including among individuals with atopic dermatitis ( Patel et
al., 2019 ) and allergic rhinitis ( Rodrigues et al., 2021 ).

The Present Study
In this study, we pooled data from the 2016 –2023 cycles of
the National Survey of Children ’s Health (NSCH) to evaluate
the associations between ACE exposure and pediatric allergic
diseases, including parent-reported allergic conditions and
asthma. Asthma was examined separately from allergic con-
ditions because, although allergic in ﬂammation contributes
importantly to asthma pathogenesis, asthma is clinically het-
erogeneous and not uniformly allergic in origin, including
nonallergic forms such as exercise-induced, virus-related, and
premenstrual asthma ( Klain et al., 2022 ). We characterized
ACE exposure both cumulatively and at the level of individual
ACE domains to examine whether associations differed across
adversity types. We also examined dose –response patterns
using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Allergic outcomes were
assessed in terms of both disease presence and parent-reported
disease severity. In addition, we assessed whether the observedassociations were modi ﬁed by key sociodemographic factors,
including child age, sex, race/ethnicity, family poverty level
(FPL), household smoking exposure, family structure, and
Study Population
Following Census Bureau guidance ( Bureau, 2024 ), this
study analyzed pooled data from the 2016 –2023 cycles of
the NSCH, a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey
of U.S. children aged 0 –17 years. Conducted by the U.S.
Census Bureau, the NSCH collects parent-reported infor-
mation on children ’s health, development, and family
environment ( Bureau, 2023 ,2024 ,2025 ;Ghandour et al.,
2018 ). One child per household is randomly selected, and
survey weights account for sampling design and nonresponse
to ensure national representativeness. Since 2016, the NSCH
has used a self-administered, mail-based format with updated
questionnaires and weighting procedures ( Ghandour et al.,
2018 ). All data are de-identi ﬁed and publicly available, and
informed consent was obtained. As shown in Figure 1 ,
children with missing information on allergic conditions,
asthma, ACEs, or any key covariates were excluded. The
ﬁnal analytical sample consisted of 290,599 participants.
Allergic Conditions and Asthma
The primary outcomes were current allergic conditions,
moderate-to-severe allergic conditions, current asthma, and
moderate-to-severe asthma. Allergic condition was de ﬁned
based on two NSCH parent-reported items: (a) “Has a doctor
or other health care provider EVER told you that this child
has allergies (such as including food, drug, insect, seasonal,
or other)? ”and (b) “If yes, does this child CURRENTLY
have the condition? ”Children whose parent or guardian
answered “Yes”to both were classi ﬁed as having a current
allergic condition. Among children with current allergic
conditions, parent-reported allergic conditions severity was
assessed using the NSCH single item: “If yes, is it mild,
moderate, or severe? ”; responses of “moderate ”or“severe ”
were classi ﬁed as moderate-to-severe allergic conditions.
Asthma was de ﬁned using parallel criteria: (a) “Has a
doctor or other health care provider EVER told you that this
child has asthma? ”and (b) “If yes, does this child
CURRENTLY have the condition? ”Current asthma was
assigned when both responses were “Yes.”Among children
with current asthma, parent-reported asthma severity was
assessed using the parallel single item: “If yes, is it mild,
moderate, or severe? ”;r e s p o n s e so f “moderate ”or“severe ”

Statistical Analysis
All analyses accounted for the NSCH ’s complex survey
design, incorporating sampling weights, strata, and primary
sampling units to yield nationally representative estimates.
For pooled analyses, annual weights were divided by the
number of survey years. Descriptive statistics included
weighted means (standard deviations) for continuous vari-
ables and unweighted counts with weighted percentages for
categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed
using weighted ttests (continuous) and χ2tests (categorical),
or the Kruskal –Wallis rank-sum test for multiple groups.
Standardized mean differences were calculated to assess
effect sizes. Time trends were visualized using bar charts
with 95% con ﬁdence intervals (CIs). Multivariable logistic
regression estimated odds ratios ( ORs) and 95% CIs for the
association between ACE scores and each allergic outcome.
Four models were constructed: Model 1, unadjusted; Model
2, adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; Model 3, addi-
tionally adjusted for region, family structure, FPL, parental
education, health insurance, and survey year; and Model 4,
fully adjusted for maternal age at child ’s birth, household
smoking, birth weight, and preterm birth. RCS models with
four knots (ACE scores: 0, 2, 4, 6) assessed potential non-
linear dose –response associations. Subgroup analyses were
conducted strati ﬁed by child age group, sex, race/ethnicity,
FPL, household smoking exposure, region, and family struc-
ture. A two-sided pvalue <.05 was considered statistically
signiﬁcant.
Transparency and Openness
This study was performed and reported in accordance with
the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in
Epidemiology reporting guideline. We reported how we
determined our sample size, all data exclusions, all measures,
and all analyses in the study. The data analyzed in this
study are publicly available from the NSCH repository. All

was on childhood exposures, prior research suggests that
stress-related risk for asthma may begin accumulating much
earlier. Maternal experiences of anxiety, depression, major
negative life events, or exposure to violence during preg-

in offspring ( Adgent et al., 2024 ;Osam et al., 2023 ). Importantly,
many adversities captured within the ACE framework are
experienced primarily by caregivers, particularly mothers, rather
than by children alone. These pers istent, family-level stressors
may serve as key pathways linking ACEs to asthma. Our ﬁndings
underscore the importance of considering both maternal and
child exposures when interpreting the impact of ACEs across
development.
Cumulative Burden and Heterogeneity of ACEs
Since it was ﬁrst proposed by Felitti et al. (1998) , the ACE
framework has emphasized the cumulative burden of early-lifeadversities, offering a polyvictimization model to quantify
stress exposure across multiple domains. In our study, we
observed a clear, dose-dependent association between ACEs
and both allergic conditions and asthma, con ﬁrmed by RCS
models. Notably, children with ≥4 ACEs had signi ﬁcantly
higher odds of allergic conditions and asthma, aligning with
previous research suggesting a threshold effect, where the
risk escalates sharply with exposure to multiple adversities
(Hamby et al., 2021 ). Existing models, including the resilience
portfolio model, underscore the cumulative and nonlinear
interactions among diverse adversities, illustrating how
the cumulative burden of trauma shapes health outcomes
(Banyard et al., 2025 ). Beyond the ACE structure, families
experiencing multiple ACEs often face additional structural
disadvantages, such as poor housing quality, limited access to
health care, and heightened maternal stress, creating a vicious
cycle that ampli ﬁes health risks. This interplay reinforces
vicious cycles of adversity, in which the accumulation of
stressors further exacerbates negative health outcomes
(Hamby et al., 2021 ). Our study indicates that this cumulative
burden and such reinforcing cycles of disadvantage may also
shape the relationship between ACE exposure and allergic
conditions.
However, when examining individual ACEs, not all ex-
posures conferred equivalent risk for allergic conditions and
asthma. Most individual ACE domains remained positively
associated with allergic outcomes after full adjustment, and
the pattern was broadly similar for the corresponding
moderate-to-severe outcomes. In particular, in our study,
caregiver mental illness and experiences of discrimination
showed the strongest associations with both allergic condi-
tions and asthma. A large U.K. primary care cohort similarly
reported increased risks of asthma and allergic rhinitis in
children exposed to common maternal mental illness, such as
depression and anxiety ( Osam et al., 2023 ). Likewise, in a
multicenter study of adolescents from racial and ethnic
minority backgrounds, perceived discrimination was linked
to both higher asthma prevalence and worse asthma control
(Thakur et al., 2017 ). It is possible that chronic adversities
such as caregiver mental illness exert more sustained biological
and environmental in ﬂuences than acute, one-time shocks like
bereavement, whose long-term impact may depend on buff-
ering resources and recovery context ( Lucente & Guidi, 2023 ;
Osam et al., 2023 ). In contrast, caregiver death exhibited
weaker and more inconsistent associations in our analysis, with
no signi ﬁcant associations remaining after covariate adjust-
ment. Although bereavement can be an intensely distressing
experience for children and may in ﬂuence long-term health
outcomes, its downstream effects may depend more heavily on
the availability of stable caregiving and a supportive envi-
ronment ( Liu et al., 2022 ).
Although precise mechanisms remain under investigation,
chronic or repeated adversity may be biologically embedded
via persistent activation of stress-response systems. ChronicAll rights, including for text and data mining, AI training, and similar technologies, are reserved.12 ZHANG, LU, WANG, GAO, AND CHENG

Developmental and Socioeconomic Vulnerability
Younger children may be more vulnerable to the impact of
ACEs, with the strongest associations observed between
ACEs and both allergic conditions and asthma in children
aged 0 –2. Notably, although the baseline prevalence of
allergic conditions and asthma was lower among younger
children, the associations between ACE exposure and parent-
reported allergic conditions or asthma were stronger in early
childhood. Early childhood is marked by immune and
microbial maturation, characterized by Th2-skewed adaptive
immunity, limited memory T/B cell reservoirs, and height-
ened sensitivity to environmental programming ( Dowling &
Levy, 2014 ;Ygberg & Nilsson, 2012 ). During this period,
adversity-induced epigenetic and neuroendocrine changes
may become biologically embedded, resulting in long-term
alterations in immune regulation and in ﬂammatory tone
(Chen & Lacey, 2018 ;Soares et al., 2021 ). Concurrently, the
infant gut microbiome undergoes rapid colonization, and its
composition plays a crucial role in shaping immune tolerance
and regulating in ﬂammatory responses ( Donald & Finlay,
2023 ;Robertson et al., 2019 ). Disruption of this process by
adverse exposures may deplete bene ﬁcial taxa and promote
proin ﬂammatory species, amplifying respiratory in ﬂamma-
tion and allergic susceptibility through the gut –lung axis
(Robertson et al., 2019 ). Importantly, greater exposure to the
home environment may also partly contribute to this pattern,
as younger children spend more time in the household setting
and may therefore be more affected by indoor allergens,
caregiver emotional distress, and other family-level adver-
sities ( Osam et al., 2023 ;Perry et al., 2024 ). Taken together,
these converging mechanisms may explain the heightened
vulnerability of younger children to the immunological
consequences of early-life adversity.
Low-income households have been consistently associated
with poorer mental and physical health in childhood ( Jones et
al., 2025 ;Wilson et al., 2025 ). In this study, the association
between ACEs and allergic conditions was stronger among
children from lower income households (FPL <100%, ≥4
ACEs, OR=2.16) than among those from higher income
households (FPL ≥400%, ≥4 ACEs, OR=1.49). Children in
low-income households are more likely to live in poorlymaintained housing and experience greater exposure to
indoor allergens such as mold, cockroaches, and rodents,
increasing their vulnerability to allergic sensitization and
asthma ( Perry et al., 2024 ). In addition, allergen-free foods are
often substantially more expensive, posing further challenges
for allergy management in resource-limited settings ( Tseng &
Vastardi, 2023 ). Notably, although no statistically signi ﬁcant
interaction was detected, strati ﬁed estimates suggested that
the association between ACEs and parent-reported asthma
remained evident across income levels and was numerically
stronger in some lower FPL strata. Indeed, prior studies have
consistently linked low income with higher asthma preva-
lence and increased emergency department utilization ( Grant
et al., 2022 ). Future studies incorporating more detailed
environmental, behavioral, and health care –related measures
are needed to better disentangle these context-speci ﬁc
mechanisms.
Implications for Practice
Allergic conditions, including asthma, may represent part
of the broader spectrum of AC E-related health outcomes.
These conditions may re ﬂect not only biological vulnera-

Limitations and Future Directions
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the
cross-sectional nature of the NSCH data precludes causal
inference, and the temporality of associations between ACEs
and allergic outcomes cannot be ﬁrmly established. Second,
both ACE exposures and allergic outcomes, including disease
severity, were reported by parents without external validation.
This single-source, single-method approach may introduce
recall or reporting bias, and common method variance could
have in ﬂated some associations. Caregiver distress or per-
ception may in ﬂuence the reporting of both adversity and
illness. However, the observed associations were not uniform
across individual ACE domains. This heterogeneity suggests
that common method variance may not fully account for the
overall pattern of ﬁndings, although it remains an important
potential source of bias. Moreover, the use of brief, caregiver-
rated severity items (mild/moderate/severe) may be subject to
subjective interpretation and misclassi ﬁcation, particularly for
conditions such as food allergy, where nonallergic sensitivities
or intolerances may be mistaken for IgE-mediated allergy. In
addition, although these items were based on prior clinician
diagnoses, variation in respondents ’understanding of terms
such as “asthma ”or“allergies ”may still have introduced
some measurement variability. Third, due to constraints of the
NSCH data, speci ﬁc subtypes such as atopic dermatitis or
allergic rhinitis could not be separately analyzed. Fourth,
parent-reported ACEs may diverge from children ’sa c t u a l
experiences, especially regarding family-related adversities.
Finally, although we adjusted for a wide range of demographic
and socioeconomic covariates, residual confounding cannot
be ruled out, particularly from unmeasured genetic or envi-
ronmental factors.
Future studies should adopt prospective designs to clarify the
temporal relationship between childhood ACEs and allergic
outcomes and to examine whether these associations persist
across developmental stages. Combining parent-reported data
with clinical information, such as physician diagnosis or
electronic health records, could improve assessment accuracy.
Using clearer diagnostic criteria and objective indicators may
enhance outcome classi ﬁcation and support interpretation
of disease severity. Finally, multi-informant approaches,
including adolescent self-reports when developmentally
appropriate, may help resolve discrepancies in reports of
family adversity and provide a more comprehensive under-
standing of ACE exposure.
Offsetting these limitations are several strengths of the
study. Leveraging a nationally representative NSCH sample
of 290,599 children, representing over 61 million U.S. chil-
dren aged 0 –17 years, this study captures a broad pediatric
population across diverse sociodemographic backgrounds. A
dose-based cumulative approach was integrated with item-
level ACE indicators to provide a more nuanced characteri-
zation of adversity in relation to allergic outcomes. Findingsremained consistent across key subgroups and were robust to
adjustment for a comprehensive set of demographic and
socioeconomic factors. Together, these features strengthen the
public health relevance of our ﬁndings and underscore the
value of integrating adversity-informed approaches into
pediatric allergy research and care.
In this nationally representative study, cumulative ACEs
were strongly and dose-dependently associated with parent-
reported allergic conditions and asthma among U.S. children,
particularly in early childhood. These ﬁndings suggest
that early-life adversity may represent an important social
determinant of pediatric allergic and respiratory health and
support early identi ﬁcation and trauma-informed approaches
to pediatric allergy and asthma care.

Posttraumatic Stress Symptoms in Australian Parents of Autistic Children:
Factor Structure of the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) and
the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for the Diagnostic and
Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (PCL-5)

Silvia Pavan1, and David Austin1, 2

Objective: Research indicates that parents of autistic children may have a higher risk of posttraumatic stress
disorder (PTSD) than parents of neurotypical children. This study was to determine the optimal factor
structure of two trauma screening assessments: the PTSD Checklist for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of
Mental Disorders ,ﬁfth edition (DSM-5 ) and the International Trauma Questionnaire, within this parent
population. Method: Conﬁrmatory factor analysis examined and compared one novel DSM-5 model and
14 previously identi ﬁedDSM-5 andInternational Classi ﬁcation of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11 ) trauma
symptom models among Australian parents of autistic children ( N=563). Results: Three DSM-5 PTSD
models (anhedonia, hybrid, and intrusion/distress) provided a marginal ﬁt, with the novel intrusion/distress
model offering a superior ﬁt. None of the ﬁveDSM-5 models tested achieved a good overall ﬁt. For ICD-11 ,
a three-factor model best ﬁt the latent structure of PTSD symptoms. For complex PTSD (CPTSD), a two-
factor second-order model and a six-factor ﬁrst-order model provided a superior ﬁt over ﬁve alternative
CPTSD models. Conclusions: Results strongly support the internal reliability and construct validity of
theICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD models in Australian parents of autistic children, as measured by the
International Trauma Questionnaire. The ICD-11 models outperformed the best DSM-5 models,
highlighting their superiority for this population. While more complex DSM-5 models showed better ﬁt than
simpler ones, they still did not achieve a good overall ﬁt. Notably, the results were largely consistent when
assessing individuals meeting Criterion A for PTSD, speci ﬁcally in relation to parenting-related traumatic
experiences. The International Trauma Questionnaire is a more reliable and suitable tool for assessing PTSD
and CPTSD in Australian parents of autistic children.
Clinical Impact Statement
This study highlights the superi or reliability and validity of the International Classi ﬁcation of Diseases
11th Revision models in assessing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex PTSD in Australian
parents of autistic children, compared with Diagnostic and Statistical Ma nual of Mental Disorders, ﬁfth
edition models. The ﬁndings support the use of the International Trauma Questionnaire as a more suitable
tool for this population, capturing th e distinct traumatic stress symptoms t hey experience. Results indicate
thatInternational Classi ﬁcation of Diseases 11th Revision models, particularly the three-factor PTSD and
six-factor complex PTSD m odels, provide a better ﬁt and clinical utility. These insights underscore the need
for trauma-informed care tailored to the unique experiences of parents in this population.

complex PTSD (CPTSD), are trauma- and stressor-related disorders
that are precipitated by exposure to a traumatic event or series of
events ( World Health Organisation, 2018 ). PTSD is characterized by
symptoms grouped into four clusters: intrusion, avoidance, negative
alterations in cognition and mood, and alterations in arousal and
reactivity. In contrast, CPTSD encompasses the core PTSD symp-
toms along with additional disturbances in self-organization (DSO),
which include affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and dif-
ﬁculties in interpersonal relationships ( Cloitre et al., 2019 ). The two
major international diagnostic classi ﬁcation systems for PTSD (the
Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, ﬁfth edition –
text rev. [DSM-5-TR ] and the International Classi ﬁcation of Diseases
11th Revision [ICD-11 ]) differ in their conceptualization of these
disorders. The DSM-5-TR focuses solely on PTSD and does not
include CPTSD as a distinct diagnosis. Instead, it incorporates
symptoms related to DSO within its alternative model for personality
disorders. In contrast, the ICD-11 explicitly distinguishes between
PTSD and CPTSD, treating them as related but separate conditions.
Evidence indicates that diagnostic rates of PTSD may vary depending
on which system is used and with which population and type of
trauma exposure ( Elliott et al., 2021 ). A number of existing self-report
measures can be used to screen for PTSD based on these systems. The
PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) and the International Trauma
Questionnaire (ITQ) have been used extensively to assess PTSD
according to DSM andICDcriteria respectively in a number of parent
samples (e.g., Karatzias et al., 2023 ;Martin et al., 2021 ;Rahimi et al.,
2023;Stewart et al., 2020 ;Sveen et al., 2016 ;Wang et al., 2019 ;
Xiong, McGrath, Stewart, et al., 2022 ;Xiong, McGrath, Yakovenko,
et al., 2022 ).
Despite widespread use, no studies to date have examined the PCL-
5 or ITQ factor structures in parent samples and more speci ﬁcally in
parents of autistic children who are known to experience higher rates of
traumatic stress symptomatology than parents of neurotypical children
(Xiong, McGrath, Yakovenko, et al., 2022 ). The elevated rates of
PTSD and CPTSD among this parent population underscore the
profound impact of cumulative parenting-related traumatic stressors.
Such experiences, which often include managing sensory sensitivities,
communication differences, and aggressive behaviors (e.g., self-harm,
suicidality, and absconding), are exacerbated by social isolation,
ﬁnancial strain, and inadequate support services ( Stephenson et al.,
2023;Xiong, McGrath, Yakovenko, et al., 2022 ). These challenges
highlight the critical need for valid and reliable assessment tools to
identify trauma in this population and guide appropriate interventions
and support. These unrelenting and cumulative stressors heighten
vulnerability to both PTSD and CPTSD, particularly in the context of
ongoing rather than single traumatic events. Despite the absence of
speciﬁc investigations into CPTSD prevalence within this population,
the unrelenting and multifaceted nature of their stressors suggests thatthis area warrants further exploration. By including CPTSD in this
study, we hope to support this line of investigation.
Investigation into the factor structure of PTSD and CPTSD
in parents of autistic children is needed to build evidence for
the clinical utility of self-report measures for assessing traumatic
stress symptoms within this unique population. As detailed below,
existing research indicates that the factor structure of both the PCL-5
and ITQ vary across populations, and as such, their appropriateness
for effectively measuring these symptoms across different popu-
lations cannot be assumed. If DSM-5 andICD-11 factor models
show a poor ﬁt in samples of parents of autistic children, then the
PCL-5 and ITQ measures may be mischaracterizing how PTSD
and/or CPTSD symptoms present in these parent populations. This
area of inquiry has implications for ongoing research in the area,
assessment and treatment of PTSD and CPTSD in clinical settings,
parent access to appropriate support and intervention, and child and
family overall well-being.
DSM-5 PTSD Models
A PTSD diagnosis based on the DSM-5-TR requires exposure to a
traumatic event ( “Criterion A ”), and endorsement of symptoms
from four core symptom clusters: reexperiencing (Criterion B),
avoidance (Criterion C), negative alterations in cognition and mood
(Criterion D), and hyperarousal (Criterion E). Numerous studies
have examined the latent structure of DSM-5 PTSD with at least
several competing models proposed. The four-factor model ( DSM-5
Model 1) is composed of the four symptom clusters mentioned
above and provides the basis for all subsequent DSM-5 models
(S. Murphy et al., 2018 ). Subsequent model iterations include the
externalizing behavior model, which separates the core alterations
in arousal and reactivity symptom cluster into three additional
symptom clusters (externalizing behavior, anxious arousal, dys-
phoric arousal) to emphasize symptoms relating to distress or
dysregulation ( Armour et al., 2016 ); the anhedonia model, which
identi ﬁes two additional distinct symptom clusters relating to
negative affect and anhedonia (seven clusters in total; Lee et al.,
2019 ). Anhedonia, or the diminished ability to experience pleasure,
is particularly relevant in trauma-related disorders as it re ﬂects the
profound impact of trauma on positive emotional experiences and
engagement with previously enjoyable activities ( Lee et al., 2019 ).
Last, the hybrid model, which emphasizes a more holistic PTSD
model, is composed of a combination of seven symptom clusters
drawn from the aforementioned models ( Gilmour & Romaniuk,
2020 ). The ﬁt of these models depends on the type of traumatic
event(s) experienced, co-occurring conditions, and the sample
characteristics in any given population ( Armour et al., 2016 ;Seo &
Cho, 2023 ). See Table 1 for the operationalization of the DSM-5
models depending on the type of trauma experienced, the char-

(“Criterion A ”), however, differs from the DSM-5-TR system in that
endorsement of one or two symptoms from only three core symptomclusters is required (reexperiencing, avoidance, and sense of currentthreat), with the addition of functional impairment. Multiple studieshave investigated the latent structure of PTSD according to the ICD-
11criteria. The ITQ has been utilized to examine the latent structure

2016;Somma et al., 2019 ;Vallires et al., 2018 ). Speci ﬁcally, three
models of the PTSD criteria have been developed and tested, with the

Comparison of DSM-5 and ICD-11 PTSD Models
Few studies to date have examined the latent structure of the two
PTSD diagnostic systems in the same samples. Those that have beenconducted indicate that there may be implications for choosing onediagnostic system over the other ( Elliott et al., 2021 ;Hansen et al.,
2017 ;Krger-Gottschalk et al., 2022 ;Tay et al., 2015 ). For example,
Hansen et al. (2017) found signi ﬁcantly higher rates of PTSD with
the PCL-5 in comparison to the ITQ for a university student sampleand almost double the rate of PCL-5 PTSD to that of the ITQ in aveteran sample. Furthermore, the results indicated that the three-factor ICD-11 model provided a satisfactory ﬁt across all sample
populations, whereas the DSM-5 model provided a marginally
acceptable ﬁt and was the poorest ﬁtting of all the models tested
(Hansen et al., 2017 ). Other studies have evaluated the ﬁt of the
DSM-5 andICD-11 using different measurement tools (e.g., Krger-
Gottschalk et al., 2022 ;Tay et al., 2015 ), and generally indicate
greater support for the ICD-11 factor structure compared with the
DSM-5 , regardless of the measurement used. These studies attrib-
uted the ICD-11 ’s superior ﬁt to its greater parsimony in form,
reduced item variation, and focus on fewer constructs. Collectively,
existing ﬁndings indicate possible risks of overestimating or un-
derestimating PTSD and CPTSD symptoms (and/or diagnosis)depending on the diagnostic criteria and measurement tool utilizedin both clinical and research spaces. For example, overestimationmay occur when tools like the PCL-5 capture a broader range ofsymptoms than those de ﬁned under ICD-11 , potentially in ﬂating
PTSD prevalence rates in certain populations. Conversely, under-estimation may arise if ICD-11 ’s narrower focus on fewer constructs
overlooks meaningful symptomatology that impacts functioning butfalls outside its diagnostic criteria. These discrepancies have sig-niﬁcant implications for accurately identifying and treating trauma-
related disorders, particularly in parenting populations, whereunique stressors may challenge traditional de ﬁnitions of trauma and
symptom thresholds.
ICD-11 CPTSD Models
For a diagnosis of CPTSD, the ICD-11 requires endorsement of
the three main PTSD symptom clusters (reexperiencing, avoidance,and sense of current threat), as well as the additional endorsement ofPOSTTRAUMATIC STRESS SYMPTOMS 1025
DSO symptom clusters (affective dysregulation, negative self-
concept, disturbances in relationships) and functional impairment.
Several studies have examined the latent structure of CPTSD
according to ICD-11 criteria. Seven different models have been
proposed with the majority of existing studies using the ITQ to
examine the CPTSD latent structure. These models are described in
A systematic review of 33 factor analytic studies indicated that of
these seven CPTSD models, both a six-factor model and a two-
factor second-order model best represented the latent structure of the
ITQ ( Redican et al., 2021 ). In addition, evidence indicates that both
models provide an optimal ﬁt across various sample populations,
with this possibly attributed to how the models capture the dis-
tinction between PTSD and DSO symptoms inherent in the for-
mulation of PTSD and CPTSD ( World Health Organisation, 2018 ).
For example, both models demonstrated a good ﬁt for a sample of
survivors of sexual violence in Ireland ( Frost et al., 2022 ), in prison
staff ( Dhingra et al., 2021 ), in veterans and combat soldiers ( Armour
et al., 2021 ;Mordeno et al., 2019 ;D. Murphy et al., 2020 ), and in
refugees ( Silove et al., 2017 ).
In light of existing literature that describes the prolonged,
repetitive nature of parenting-related traumatic events experienced
by some parents of autistic children ( Stewart et al., 2020 ), CPTSD
clinical conceptualization (as measured by the ITQ) is likely to be
present in some or many cases. Therefore, further examination of the
construct validity in self-report of related symptoms is warranted in
this parent population.
The Present Study
The aim of the present study was to evaluate the ﬁt of a number of
DSM-5 andICD-11 factorial models of both PTSD ( DSM andICD)
and CPTSD ( ICD) in a sample of parents of autistic children, as
measured by the PCL-5 and the ITQ. These measures were selected
due to their robust psychometric properties, their alignment with
DSM-5 andICD-11 diagnostic frameworks, and their suitability for
online administration. The PCL-5 is well-established for assessing
DSM-5 PTSD symptoms, while the ITQ is speci ﬁcally designed for
ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD criteria. To assess Criterion A (exposure
to traumatic events), the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5)
was used. The LEC-5 was chosen for its brevity, validation in trauma
research, and compatibility with the PCL-5. Alternative tools, such as
the Trauma History Questionnaire or the Posttraumatic Diagnostic
Scale–5, were excluded due to concerns about length and suitability
for an online survey format. In addition to analysis of the full sample,
a subset analysis was conducted on participants who met Criterion A.
This subset analysis was included to assess whether meeting Criterion
A, a prerequisite for PTSD diagnosis, in ﬂuenced the ﬁto ft h et e s t e d
models. The results of this analysis provide additional insights into
the applicability of DSM-5 andICD-11 models for PTSD and CPTSD
in this population.
A novel aspect of this study was that the responses to the PCL-5
and ITQ were in relation to self-report parenting-speci ﬁc experi-
ences, and not in response to generic traumatic stressors listed in
standardized measurement scales such as the LEC-5. No studies
have examined the DSM-5 and/or ICD-11 model ﬁt in parents of
autistic children, therefore, the DSM-5 andICD-11 model selection
for this study was based on the ﬁndings of previous studies on a

Procedure and Participants
An online cross-sectional survey was conducted in Australia
targeting parents of autistic and neurotypical children. Eligibilityrequired parents to be at least 18 years old, residing in Australia,with a child aged 17 or younger. This age limit was chosen to focuson parents actively engaged in caregiving roles, as the demands ofparenting are particularly acute during childhood and adolescence.This stage often involves heightened stressors, especially for parentsof autistic children, related to education, behavior, and health careneeds ( Stephenson et al., 2023 ).
Parents of autistic children were required to con ﬁrm in the online
survey whether their child had received a formal autism diagnosis byanswering “yes”or“no.”Participants were also asked to specify the
type of autism diagnosis (e.g., Autism Spectrum Disorder, Asperger ’s
Disorder) and the child ’s age at the time the diagnosis was made. Data
from parents of neurotypical children were collected as part of a larger
study, where they served as a control group. They are not reported on
in this article as the research aims and analyses do not require acontrol group. Participants were recruited nationwide through variousmethods, including emails to autism organizations, social media ads,online forums, support groups, health clinics, and community noticeboards. Snowball sampling was also employed, allowing participantsto share the survey with other parents. Participants did not receivemonetary compensation for their participation; however, they weregiven the option to enter a prize draw to win one of four noise-canceling headsets.
Participants were eligible for inclusion in the study if they resided
in Australia, were the parent of a child aged 17 years or younger, andhad a child with a formal autism diagnosis if participating as a parentof an autistic child. Participants were excluded if they did not residein Australia, had a child older than 17 years, did not have a child witha formal autism diagnosis (if participating as a parent of an autisticchild), had a history of severe cognitive impairment or neurologicaldisorder that could impact questionnaire comprehension, or had a
history of signi ﬁcant substance use disorder that could interfere with
symptom reporting. Participants completed three questionnaires(LEC-5, ITQ, and PCL-5) in a ﬁxed order: LEC-5 ﬁrst, followed by
ITQ, and then PCL-5. This order ensured that participants anchoredtheir responses to a speci ﬁc traumatic event identi ﬁed in the LEC-5.
Participants were instructed to refer to the same event for both theITQ and PCL-5. While the possibility of order effects cannot beruled out, this sequence minimized ambiguity and maintained clarityin symptom reporting.
This study composed of 563 parents of autistic children. On
average, parents were around 43.1 years old, with children averaging11.5 years. The majority of parents were female (84.6%), and mothers(84.4%), and a signi ﬁcant proportion were in married or domestic
partnerships (72.6%). Household composition varied, with 67.1%having both biological parents, while 28.4% were single biological
parents. The ethnic makeup was primarily Caucasian or white
European (88.9%). A notable portion of the parents held universitydegrees or higher (54.4%), and employment was distributed acrossfull-time (29.7%), part-time (36.1%), and stay-at-home parenting(22.3%). In terms of income, 42.1% reported earnings over $100,000,while 31.3% had less than $74,000. Most families had two children(49.2%) in the household. Participants completed part two of the LEC-5, in which theyreported their most challenging parenting-related event/s (traumaticevent) and additional questions relating to this event. LEC-5 Part Two
The LEC-5 (Part 2) was used to assess participants ’exposure to
potentially traumatic parenting-related experiences ( Weathers et al.,
2013 ). The wording was modi ﬁed from the original version of the
measure such that the instruction, “brieﬂy describe the worst event, ”
was instead presented as “brieﬂy identify the worst event in par-
enting your autistic child. ”This was done to orient participants to
their most challenging parenting experience rather than any othernonparenting experiences. The LEC-5 (Part 2) used in this studyconsisted of seven items that asked the participant to ﬁrst give a brief
description of a very stressful parenting event/s, when the eventoccurred, how it was experienced, if the event involved actual orthreatened death, serious injury, and number of exposures to similarevents. The item on sexual violence was excluded due to the absenceof evidence of such incidents by autistic children toward parents,streamlining the survey to reduce participant burden. This exclusionis discussed as a possible limitation. No other adaptions to theinstructions or to the LEC-5 items were made.

PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)
The PCL-5 ( Weathers et al., 2013 ) is a 20-item self-report measure
assessing PTSD severity referencing the past month and was used to
measure symptoms according to the DSM-5 criteria of PTSD. The
PCL-5 references four domains (reexperiencing, avoidance, negative
alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal) that are con-
sistent with the four PTSD criteria of the DSM-5 . For the purposes of
this project, the original PCL-5 instructions were adapted to directparents to respond to “thinking about your worst event in parenting
your autistic child you listed previously (i.e., from the LEC-5), which
for this questionnaire means the event or events that currently bother
you the most. ”No other adaptions to the instructions or to the
PCL-5 items were made. Participants rated the intensity of their
symptom experience on a 5-point Likert scale (0 =not at all ,4=
extremely ). In this study, PCL-5 demonstrated acceptable reliability
The ITQ ( Cloitre et al., 2019 ) was used to measure symptoms
according to the ICD-11 criteria for PTSD and CPTSD. The ITQ
PTSD and CPTSD subscales each comprise three core symptom
clusters (PTSD: reexperiencing, avoidance, sense of threat; CPTSD:
affective dysregulation, negative self-concept, disturbances in
relationships) each assessed by two items. For the purposes of this
project, the original ITQ instructions were adapted to direct parti-
cipants to identify and report “a very stressful parenting experience/s
with your autistic child …and to indicate how much you have been
bothered by that problem in the past month. ”No other adaptions to
the instructions or to the ITQ items were made. Participants rated
the intensity of their symptom experience on a 5-point Likert scale
(0=not at all ,4=extremely ). In this study, the ITQ showed
excellent reliability ( α=.94).
Data Analysis
Data screening was conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version
29; IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York), and the CFA models
were constructed and tested in IBM SPSS AMOS (Version 28).
Little ’s test indicated that less than 1% of the data was missing across
both PCL-5 and ITQ scales, so mean replacement was used to
replace all missing values. This approach was deemed appropriate
due to the low proportion of missing data, which reduces the
potential bias that could be introduced by more complex imputation
methods such as multiple imputation. All item scores were within
appropriate ranges. The item mappings of the four CFA models are
shown in Tables 1 and2. As the PCL-5 and ICD-11 scores were
found to have violations to the assumptions of univariate and
multivariate normality, the Bollen –Stine bootstrap χ2test was used to
assess signi ﬁcance of model ﬁt(Bollen & Stine, 1992 ). In addition,
the following global ﬁt indices were used to determine model ﬁt for
each CFA: chi-square value ( χ2;Hayduk, 2014a ), comparative ﬁt
index (CFI), root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA),
and the standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR). A good ﬁt
to the data was determined by a nonsigni ﬁcant chi-square minimum
(CMIN) value, a CMIN of ≤5, a CFI of 0.95, and RMSEA and
SRMR values of 0.08 or less ( Hu & Bentler, 1999 ;Kline, 2022 ;
Norris, 1990 ;West et al., 2012 ). Due to the exploratory nature of this
research a slightly lower Cronbach ’sα(.60) was deemed as an
acceptable measure of internal reliability ( Kline, 1999 ).

This study was the ﬁrst to assess the reliability and ﬁt of several
factorial PTSD and CPTSD models using the PCL-5 and the ITQ in
a sample of parents of autistic children; a population known to
experience higher rates of traumatic parenting experiences and
symptoms than parents of typically developing children ( Stewart et
al., 2020 ). For the PCL-5, two of the ﬁve models adequately ﬁt the
data which was based on: a borderline ﬁt in some of the goodness-
of-ﬁt indicators (e.g., CMIN, CFI, RMSEA), and signi ﬁcant chi-
square values (( Hayduk, 2014a ,2014b ). For the ITQ, the three-
factor PTSD model and both the six-factor ﬁrst-order and two-factorsecond-order CPTSD models based on ICD-11 criteria showed the
bestﬁt to the data. Notably, Model 5 provided a statistically sig-
niﬁcant improvement in ﬁt over Model 3. However, concerns about
overﬁtting and multicollinearity arose in Model 5, with correlations
exceeding 1.00 in the training set and original data set. These
ﬁndings suggest that while Model 5 demonstrates superior statistical
ﬁt, Model 3 offers a more conceptually valid structure, as it avoids
the issues of over ﬁtting. It is important to caution that over ﬁtting in
Model 5 may indicate that the model is too speci ﬁc to this data set
and may not generalize well to other samples. Therefore, further
testing across different data sets is required to ensure the robustness
and applicability of this model. Overall, a good representation of the
latent structure of the ITQ was found in this parent sample, with a
trade-off between statistical ﬁt and conceptual clarity.
This suggests that the ICD-11 models, even the worst among them,
outperform the best DSM-5 models, indicating a superior represen-
tation of PTSD and CPTSD in this parent population. Further support
for this ﬁnding comes from a subset analysis of participants meeting
Criterion A for PTSD ( n=204), which also showed ICD-11 models
demonstrating superior ﬁt compared with DSM-5 models. Both of the
best-ﬁtting ITQ CPTSD models captured the distinction between

Further, the ITQ scale scores demonstrated acceptable levels of
internal reliability, with the exception of affective dysregulation.This inadequate reliability was also observed by both Frost et al.
(2022) andSele et al. (2020) , who attributed it to the presence of two
opposing types of emotion regulation coping strategies (i.e., hyper-and hypoaffective states) one of which may be preferred more thanthe other. This theory provides a plausible explanation for the lowerreliability observed in the affective dysregulation subscale in thisstudy and aligns with existing literature on emotional regulationvariability. However, further research is needed to clarify the role ofthese opposing coping strategies and their in ﬂuence on the scale ’s
reliability.
Despite this, all factor loadings for both models were positively
and statistically signi ﬁcant, with mostly moderate to strong load-
ings, indicating that the relationships between the latent factors andthe observed variables were substantial and statistically reliable.
This implies that both models provide a close approximation of the
sample data and represent viable models of the latent PTSD andCPTSD structure of the ITQ. Furthermore, indicating that the ITQmay be an acceptable standardized measurement tool to assessparenting experiences for PTSD and CPTSD speci ﬁcally in parents
of autistic children in both research and clinical contexts. Thisfurther underscores the robustness of the ICD-11 criteria and the
ITQ as superior tools for assessing PTSD and CPTSD in this parentpopulation.
It was interesting to note that only two of the DSM-5 models
provided an adequate ﬁt to the data given the broader symptom
coverage that is inherent in this system ( Hansen et al., 2017 ). The
intrusion/distress model did show a superior ﬁt of the more well-
established DSM-5 PTSD models; however, an overall good ﬁt was
still not demonstrated for this population. The ﬁt of this model may
have been impacted by lower standardized factor loadings occurringfor two scale items (recklessness and trauma-related amnesia) andmay suggest that item modi ﬁcation (e.g., parameter changes, and
item adjustment) may improve the PCL-5 factor structure.
Ourﬁndings clearly indicate that the ICD-11 PTSD criteria, as
assessed by the ITQ, offer a more appropriate and reliable frame-work for trauma assessment and intervention in Australian parentsof autistic children compared with the DSM-5 . While these results
align with the majority of ﬁndings from studies utilizing the
ITQ/ ICD framework, they also highlight unique aspects of trauma
experienced by this speci ﬁc population. The better ﬁto fICD-11
models over DSM-5 models may also be in ﬂuenced by speci ﬁc
sociocultural factors related to our Australian sample. The ongoingcaregiving stressors faced by parents of autistic children, such associal isolation, limited support services, and ﬁnancial strain, might
be particularly pronounced in Australia, potentially shaping howPTSD and CPTSD symptoms cluster in this population. The chronicand cumulative nature of these stressors aligns more closely with
ICD-11 ’s broader framework, which distinguishes between PTSD
and CPTSD. As such, this sample ’s unique experience may con-
tribute to the observed superiority of ICD-11 models, suggesting
that sociocultural context plays an important role in shaping traumaresponses in this parent population.
In particular, our results indicate that the self-report symptoms of
PTSD do not appear to be represented by the traditional four-factormodel (intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognitions andmood, arousal and reactivity) in parents, and instead have a com-plexity that is best understood when looking at PTSD alongsidesymptoms of DSO (e.g., feeling like a failure, worthless, distant or
cutoff, and dif ﬁculties with emotional closeness). Trauma informed
intervention has been reported to appropriately treat symptoms ofDSO (e.g., Purnell et al., 2021 ). This may suggest the need for
clinicians working with some parents of autistic children to utilize
trauma-focused interventions. From a research perspective, futurestudies investigating the ( Norris, 1990 ) mental health of this parent
population may bene ﬁt from considering PTSD and CPTSD con-
ceptualizations in addition to anxiety, depression, and stress.
Limitations and Future Research
A limitation of this study was the fact that only two posttraumatic
stress measures were compared. Other scales (e.g., Traumatic StressSchedule, Norris, 1990 ; and Traumatic Events Questionnaire,
(Hayduk, 2014a ,2014b ;Norris, 1990 ;Vrana & Lauterbach, 1994 )
might also provide adequate conceptualizations of PTSD andCPTSD in this parent population. Additionally, the use of a con-venience sample may have introduced bias, limiting the gener-alizability of the ﬁndings.
The exclusion of the sexual assault item from the LEC-5. Although
sexual assaults are highly correlated with PTSD, this item wasomitted due to the minimal research connecting it to the experience ofparenting trauma in raising autistic children. However, this omissionmay have in ﬂuenced the overall results, as individuals who experi-
enced sexual assault as their traumatic event might not have endorseda Criterion A event.
The potential con ﬂation of trauma, stress, and challenging events in
the full sample was also a consideration. Further analyses isolatingparticipants who met full diagnostic criteria for PTSD or CPTSD,including Criterion A for trauma exposure, demonstrated consistentpsychometric properties within this subgroup. This ﬁnding addresses
concerns regarding potential con ﬂation and reinforces the robustness
of our ﬁndings.
Furthermore, this study relied solely on self-report measures which
can lead to discrepancies between reported symptoms those identi ﬁed
in clinical interviews. This reliance on self-report may have affectedthe reported prevalence and presentation of PTSD. Future researchshould consider complementing self-report measures with structuredclinical interviews to provide a more comprehensive understanding ofPTSD symptoms in this population.
Finally, it is important to highlight that this study did not assess
the diagnostic accuracy of the PTSD measures used in this speci ﬁc
population. While tools provided valuable insights into PTSD andCPTSD prevalence, their accuracy in diagnosing these conditionsamong parents of autistic children remains untested.
Future studies should explore a broader range of trauma measures
beyond the PCL-5 and ITQ to capture a more comprehensiveunderstanding of PTSD and CPTSD in parents of autistic children.This could include scales such as the Traumatic Stress Schedule andthe Traumatic Events Questionnaire. Additionally, to enhance thegeneralizability of ﬁndings, future research should employ random
sampling methods rather than convenience sampling, ensuring amore representative sample of the parent population. Furthermore, itis crucial to further investigate the role of DSO symptoms in themanifestation of PTSD and CPTSD among parents of autisticchildren. The strong representation of DSO in the ICD-11 models
and the inadequacy of traditional PTSD models in capturing thecomplexity of symptoms in this population suggest that a deeper1032 HINDE ET AL.
understanding of DSO could inform the development of more
targeted and effective therapeutic strategies.
The present study is the ﬁrst to our knowledge to examine the ﬁt
of data from a population of Australian parents of autistic children tovarious DSM-5 and ICD-11 models of PTSD and CPTSD,
respectively, as measured by the ITQ and the PCL-5. Empiricalsupport was found for the three-factor ICD-11 PTSD model, as well
as the six-factor ﬁrst-order and the two-factor second-order ICD-11
CPTSD models when using the ITQ, suggesting that the factorstructure of the ITQ may be a valid screening assessment for PTSDand CPTSD in this parent population. Additional analysis of Models3 and 5 revealed that Model 5 provided a signi ﬁcantly better sta-
tistical ﬁt; however, concerns over multicollinearity and over ﬁtting,
indicated by correlations exceeding 1.00 in the training set andoriginal data set, suggesting that Model 3 may offer a more con-ceptually valid structure. While Model 3 captures a better statisticalﬁt, the trade-off with Model 3 ’s conceptual clarity should be
considered. Two of the DSM-5 models tested using the PCL-5
showed an adequate ﬁt to the data with the remaining three models
providing a poor ﬁt. The novel intrusion/distress model showed a

ICD-11 PTSD, Complex PTSD, and Substance Use Disorder:
Associations and Clinical Correlates
Logan R. Harvey1, 2, Tim Slade1, Christina Marel1, and Katherine L. Mills1
1The Matilda Centre, The University of Sydney
2Drug Health, Western Sydney Local Health District, Sydney, Australia
Objective: Trauma- and substance-related disorders are highly comorbid. The co-occurrence of these
conditions is associated with a more severe clinical pro ﬁle in patients, and this remains a signi ﬁcant
challenge for treatment providers. Previous research examining this comorbidity has focused on
Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, posttraumatic stress disorder
(PTSD), resulting in a signi ﬁcant lack of research examining other formulations of trauma-related
disorders, in particular complex posttraumatic stress disorder (CPTSD). It remains unclear whether
CPTSD requires additional treatment approaches in substance use treatment settings. The present study
sought to expand previous research by examining the International Classi ﬁcation of Diseases
11th revision PTSD and Complex PTSD (CPTSD) diagnoses in a substance use treatment setting, and to
explore the associations between symptoms of PTSD/CPTSD and characteristics of substance use
disorder. Method: The study utilized data collected from individuals commencing substance use
treatment ( n=72). Assessments included trauma exposure, trauma-related symptoms, primary substance
of concern, psychological dependence, and recent substance use. Results: Forty percent of the sample
screened positive for a trauma-related disorder, with CPTSD more prevalent (34.8%) than PTSD (5.8%).
There was limited evidence of associations between trauma-related symptoms and substance use
characteristics. Severity of each CPTSD symptom cluster (affect dysregulation, negative self-concept,
disturbances in relationships) was signi ﬁcantly, positively associated with psychological dependence, a
pattern not found for PTSD symptoms. Conclusions: Given that CPTSD is more prevalent and
demonstrates differential relationships to substance use characteristics, future research should examine
whether individuals with comorbid CPTSD would bene ﬁt from adjusted treatment approaches.
Clinical Impact Statement
Individuals presenting for substance use treatment demonstrate high levels of exposure to traumatic
events, and an increased prevalence of trauma-related disorders. Complex posttraumatic stress disorder
(CPTSD) is more prevalent than PTSD in this population. There appear to be differential associations
between symptoms of PTSD versus CPTSD and psychological dependence to a substance. The
additional symptoms associated with CPTSD should be considered in treatment —including via direct
screening at assessment. Existing, evidence-based treatments should be adapted to address the additional
CPTSD-related symptoms within substance use treatment.

disorders, addiction

For individuals presenting for treatment of substance use disorder
(SUD), trauma-related disorders are a prevalent comorbidity. InAustralia, it is estimated that between 5 and 66% of those presentingfor substance use treatment will meet criteria for current posttrau-matic stress disorder ( Kingston et al., 2017 ) with the predominance
of studies deriving estimates that are considerably higher than thoseobserved in the general population (4.4%; McEvoy et al., 2011 ).
Individuals presenting with this comorbidity are more likely toexperience additional comorbid mental and physical health concerns(Bowe & Rosenheck, 2015 ;Prior et al., 2017 ), greater severity of
SUD ( Patel et al., 2021 ), and more signi ﬁcant functional and
psychosocial impairment ( L. R. Cohen & Hien, 2006 ;Drapkin et al.,
2011). Consistent with this, PTSD has been identi ﬁed as a key
treatment challenge for substance use treatment providers ( Gielen
et al., 2014 ;Killeen et al., 2015 ;Mefodeva et al., 2023 ;Roberts
et al., 2023 ).
The co-occurrence of SUD and PTSD has most commonly been
understood in terms of the self-medication hypothesis —the theory
that substance use functions to relieve symptoms of PTSD ( Khantzian,
1997). In line with this, symptoms of PTSD and SUD are mutually
reinforcing ( Tripp et al., 2020 ), and individuals with this comorbidity
experience cravings in response to both trauma- and substance-relatedcues ( Coffey et al., 2002 ). Individuals with co-occurring PTSD and
SUD experience worse clinical outcomes compared with those withsingle diagnoses, particularly in relation to functional and psychosocialimpairments ( L. R. Cohen & Hien, 2006 ;Najt et al., 2011 ). Effective
treatments do exist; however, these demonstrate high levels of attrition(Hien et al., 2023 ;Roberts et al., 2022 ). These treatments typically
involve the parallel or integrated targeting of both PTSD- and SUD-related symptoms. In general, trauma-focused interventions areeffective in the presence of co-occurring substance use and there isevidence that reductions in trauma-related symptoms correspondto reductions in SUD-related symptoms —highlighting the sig-
niﬁcance of targeting this comorbidity within SUD treatment
(Back et al., 2014 ;Hien et al., 2010 ,2018 ;Kaczkurkin et al., 2016 ;
Roberts et al., 2023 ). The available literature indicates that pathways
to recovery from substance use are often nonlinear, and there havebeen limited ﬁndings to date which help identify individuals who
might be treatment responders versus those who do not ( Lopez-Castro
et al., 2015 ). Given the increased symptom severity and high rates
of treatment attrition in this presentation, it remains important tofurther understand the relationship between PTSD and SUD to informreﬁnements to treatment options.
The majority of studies that have examined this relationship have
focused on hyperarousal symptoms. The Present Study
The aim of the present study then, was to expand previous
research by ﬁrst examining the prevalence of ICD-11 PTSD and
CPTSD in a substance use treatment setting, and second to explorethe associations between ICD-11 trauma-related symptom clusters
and characteristics of SUD (primary substance of concern, recentsubstance use, and psychological dependence). As most previousresearch has focused on substance use behavior or on speci ﬁc
substances, the present study will additionally examine psycho-logical dependence to a substance. Psychological dependence is animportant characteristic of substance use disorders and re ﬂects the
nonphysiological components of dependence to a psychoactivesubstance ( Raistrick et al., 1994 ). This component of dependence
is reﬂected in diagnostic formulations for substance-related con-
ditions across both DSM andICD systems.
Materials and Method
The present study analyzed data from the Trauma and Substance
Use in Treatment study, a prospective cohort study of 72 peoplerecruited from a large, public metropolitan substance use treatment
service based in greater Sydney, Australia. Ethics approval wasobtained from the Western Sydney Local Health District HumanResearch Ethics Committee (HREC/18/WMEAD/441).
Participants were recruited as they entered substance use treat-
ment. All eligible patients entering treatment with a counselor orpsychologist for substance use issues were invited to participate.
Eligibility criteria were as follows:(a) aged 18 years or over; (b) Australian citizen or permanent
resident; (c) ﬂuent in English; and (d) agreed to provide contact
details for follow-up.
Participants were administered a series of measures by their
treating clinician in their initial clinical assessment sessions. Thesemeasures assessed demographics, substance use (past month), traumaexposure (lifetime), and trauma symptoms (past month).
Demographics
Demographics included participant age (years) and gender
(“male, ”“female, ”“transgender, ”“do not identify as female, male
or transgender, ”or“prefer not to say ”).
Substance Use
Substance use characteristics were assessed as part of routine
data collection upon treatment entry using the NSW Health Drugand Alcohol Clinical Assessment and the Australian TreatmentOutcomes Pro ﬁle (Ryan et al., 2014 ). This included self-identi ﬁed
primary substance of concern and secondary substance of concern(categorized as “alcohol, ”“cannabis, ”or“other illicit substance, ”
the latter group consisting of cocaine, heroin, and methamphet-amine); and the number of days in the past month they had used theirprimary substance (maximum of 28 days). The Leeds DependenceQuestionnaire (LDQ; Raistrick et al., 1994 ) was used to assess
psychological dependence in relation to primary substance of concern.The LDQ is a 10-item self-report measure of psychological depen-dence to psychoactive substances. Scores range from 0 to 30, withhigher scores indicating a greater level of psychological dependence.The LDQ has been found to have sound psychometric properties,including reliability, validity, and sensitivity to change ( Heather et al.,
2001;Raistrick et al., 2014 ). In the present study, the LDQ demon-
strated sound reliability ( α=.93).
Trauma, PTSD, and CPTSD
The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) was used to assess
trauma exposure speci ﬁcally in childhood ( Bernstein & Fink,
1998 ). The CTQ is a 28-item measure that assesses exposure to ﬁve
domains of childhood maltreatment: physical abuse, sexual abuse,emotional abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. Scoresare summed for each domain, with higher scores re ﬂecting more
severe levels of maltreatment. Endorsement of each domain ofchildhood maltreatment was calculated based on the suggested cutscores for the measure ( Bernstein & Fink, 1998 ). The Life Events
Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC5; Weathers et al., 2013 ) was admin-
istered to assess trauma exposure more broadly. In considering1038 HARVEY, SLADE, MAREL, AND MILLS
exposure to LEC-5 —only those directly experienced were considered
for this particular study. The International Trauma Questionnaire
(ITQ; Cloitre et al., 2018 ) was used to assess symptoms of ICD-11
PTSD and CPTSD. Participant responses to the ITQ were scored as
per the recommended diagnostic algorithm ( Cloitre et al., 2018 ).
Speci ﬁcally, participants who endorsed an individual symptom at
“moderately ”or higher on at least one item assessing the symptom
were considered to endorse the symptom. For participants to screen
positive for ICD-11 PTSD, they were required to endorse one or more
symptoms from each of the three PTSD core symptom clusters (re-
experiencing, avoidance, and persistent sense of threat) and functional
impairment related to those symptoms. For ICD-11 CPTSD, parti-
cipants were required to meet criteria for the PTSD diagnosis and
additionally endorse one or more symptoms from each of the three
CPTSD symptom clusters (affect regulation issues, negative self-
concept, and interpersonal issues) and functional impairment related
to those symptoms. A summed score for each symptom cluster was
calculated to act as a measure of symptom severity. The ITQ has been
validated in a range of populations and settings and demonstrates
sound psychometric properties ( Redican et al., 2021 ). In the present
study, the ITQ demonstrated sound reliability for the full scale ( α=
.94) PTSD scale ( α=.82) and CPTSD subscale ( α=.88).
Data Analysis
There was a small amount ( <1%) of missing data across the survey
questions. Numbers are reported alongside proportions for categorical
variables broken down by group for demographic, substance use, and
trauma variables. The distributions of all continuous variables to be
examined inferentially were found to deviate from normality, with
signiﬁcant issues with skewness evident as assessed with the Shapiro –
Wilk and Kolmogorov –Smirnov tests and by visual inspection of
Q–Q plots. Medians are reported with interquartile ranges (IQR) for
continuous variables. Inferential analyses were carried out using
median scores primarily, however, means and standard deviations are
additionally reported for key study variables to allow comparison to
the broader literature.
To evaluate the associations between primary substance groups
and the trauma-related symptom clusters, Kruskal –Wallis and
Mann–Whitney tests were utilized. For the Kruskal –Wallis test,
effect sizes were calculated using η-squared calculated for the H
statistic ( η2;B. H. Cohen, 2008 ;Tomczak & Tomczak, 2014 ). This
effect size re ﬂects the variance in mean rank accounted for by the
group variable, analogous to the eta-squared used within a simple
analysis of variance ( B. H. Cohen, 2008 ). These effect sizes were
interpreted with the available convention for the standard η2based
on the fstatistics, where small =.01, medium =.06, and large =.14
(J. Cohen, 1988 ). Pairwise comparisons were then conducted with
Mann–Whitney Utests utilizing a Bonferroni correction for
multiple comparisons, with Glass ’s rank-biserial correlation ( rG)
used to estimate effect sizes ( Glass, 1966 ;Tomczak & Tomczak,
2014 ). This effect size provides a measure of the difference in
average rank between two groups and is similar to the Pearson r
statistic ( B. H. Cohen, 2008 ). These effect sizes were interpreted
based on the available conventions, with small =.1, medium =.3,
and large =.5 (J. Cohen, 1988 ). To assess associations between
symptom clusters and the other substance use characteristics (recent
substance use, psychological dependence), correlation coef ﬁcients
were calculated using Spearman ’s rank-order correlation ( rs).Results

This study was one of the ﬁrst to examine the prevalence and
clinical correlates of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD in a population
seeking substance use treatment. As expected, the sample dem-
onstrated high rates of exposure to traumatic experiences across
the lifespan. Unsurprisingly, there were correspondingly high rates
of trauma-related symptoms identi ﬁed. Rates of endorsement for
each of the ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD symptoms were above 50%,
and over 40% of the sample screened positive for either trauma-
related disorder. Of speci ﬁc importance, rates of CPTSD were
found to be considerably higher than PTSD. There are no previ-
ously published rates of ICD-11 PTSD or CPTSD in individuals
presenting for substance use treatment by which the current results
could be better understood. Broadly, the overall prevalence of
40.6% of the sample screening positive for either disorder is in line
with earlier prevalence rates of PTSD in comparable samples
(Kingston et al., 2017 ). The ﬁnding that the majority of these
individuals (85.7%) re ﬂected a CPTSD rather than a PTSD pro ﬁle
is signi ﬁcant and suggests that CPTSD is a particularly salient
clinical issue in substance use treatment. This ﬁnding is consistent
with other studies conducted among clinical samples. For example,
previous research in individuals seeking trauma-related treatment
at a public psychiatric service in Scotland found that 75.6% of
individuals re ﬂected a CPTSD pro ﬁle, and only 24.4% endorsed
aP T S Dp r o ﬁle (Karatzias et al., 2017 ). The rates of CPTSD
symptoms present in the current sample provide an important
indicator for clinicians that as sessment of posttraumatic psycho-
pathology needs to be expanded beyond the well-established
traditional symptoms of standard PTSD.

additional treatment needs for those experiencing CPTSD symptoms.

Limitations
There are several limitations that should be considered when
reviewing the current results. Primarily, the emergence of a globalpandemic and the redirection of health service resources, necessi-tated the premature cessation of recruitment for the study. Thesubsequent sample size will have in ﬂuenced statistical power and
the generalizability of results. In particular, the small sample andunequal group sizes precluded group comparisons for the PTSDand CPTSD groups. The present study is also only able to providescreened estimates of prevalence rates for PTSD and CPTSD, as nodiagnostic con ﬁrmation was obtained via clinical interview. There
are, however, strong suggestions that diagnostic con ﬁrmation via
clinical interview should not always be considered superior in thisendeavor ( Hyland & Shevlin, 2024 ). There remains a strong need for
further research in this area. Given the complexities of collectingdata in under resourced, frontline services, it is important that futureresearch employ mechanisms to maximize the utility of data col-lected. The examination of a broader range of substance use char-acteristics, including primary substances of concern, will allowmore detailed analysis of the relationships between trauma- andsubstance-related disorders.
In conclusion, the present study con ﬁrms the high prevalence
of trauma exposure in individuals seeking treatment for substanceuse problems but also expands the literature by providing the ﬁrst
estimates of the rates of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD in this
population. These results suggest that CPTSD is a potentiallyimportant comorbid diagnosis in SUD treatment requiring furtherexamination.
